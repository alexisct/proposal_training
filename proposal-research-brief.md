# Research Brief: What Makes a Good Academic Funding Proposal

Research date: 4 May 2026

## Executive Summary

Strong academic funding proposals usually do five things well:

1. They make a clear, important, and original case for the research question.
2. They show a methodologically credible plan that is feasible within the time, team, and resources available.
3. They demonstrate strong fit between aims, methods, team, environment, and budget.
4. They explain why the project matters now, and who or what benefits from it.
5. They are written for assessors, not just for subject specialists: clear structure, explicit logic, and no missing steps.

For the current UKRI NERC `Pushing the frontiers of environmental research` opportunity, those general strengths still apply, but the scheme places extra weight on whether the project is genuinely adventurous, ambitious, and curiosity-driven, while still remaining credible and well designed. The current opportunity is the always-open version launched on `31 March 2026`; this is different from the previous deadline-based `January 2026` round that closed on `21 January 2026`.

## General Principles

### 1. Funder Fit Comes First

A good proposal is not just a strong research idea. It must visibly match the funder’s aims and assessment framework.

UKRI’s own core application guidance is a good generic model for what funders look for in applicant-led schemes:

- `Vision`: quality, importance, novelty, timeliness, and potential to advance understanding
- `Approach`: effectiveness, feasibility, methodology, risk management, and route from outputs to outcomes
- `Applicant and team capability to deliver`: the right skills, leadership, and environment
- `Resources and cost justification`: appropriate and justified use of funds
- `Ethics and RRI`: responsible design, delivery, and management

Inference: even outside UKRI, these are very close to the universal questions most research funders ask, whether they use those exact headings or not.

### 2. The Best Proposals Are Both Informative and Persuasive

Cambridge’s Research Operations Office guidance captures the core balance well: proposals should explain how the project meets funder aims, why it matters, why the approach is effective, why the research is exciting and original, and what the likely issues are and how they will be handled.

That translates into a simple reviewer test:

- Is the problem worth funding?
- Is this the right team and design?
- Will this project actually work?
- Is the proposal stronger than the many other very good ones?

### 3. Originality Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

Originality matters, but weakly specified “novelty” is not enough. Good proposals make novelty legible by showing:

- what gap or limitation in current understanding exists
- what the proposed work changes
- why the advance is meaningful rather than incremental

The University of Edinburgh’s guidance is useful here: strong proposals should identify a clear research gap, make a significant contribution to knowledge, explain why the work is important or timely, and articulate wider benefit.

### 4. Feasibility Is a Competitive Strength, Not a Bureaucratic Add-On

Across sources, feasibility repeatedly matters:

- objectives should be realistic
- methods should be appropriate and transparent
- risks should be identified and managed
- timelines and milestones should make sense
- resources should match the plan

Weak proposals often fail not because the idea is uninteresting, but because the assessors cannot see a convincing route from question to delivery.

### 5. Team and Environment Must Be Part of the Argument

Funders are not only assessing a project abstractly; they are assessing whether this team can deliver it in this setting.

Strong proposals therefore show:

- the necessary mix of expertise
- credible leadership and management
- access to facilities, data, infrastructure, or collaborations
- a research environment that genuinely supports delivery

For UKRI specifically, the team case should not be written as a conventional CV dump. It should be evidence-led and tied directly to delivery of the proposed work.

### 6. Clarity Is Not Cosmetic

A confusing proposal is often treated as a weaker proposal. General guidance from UKRI and universities consistently points toward:

- explicit structure
- short, direct prose
- visible alignment between question, objectives, method, and outcomes
- careful use of visuals only where they genuinely help
- self-contained writing rather than relying on external links

Inference: clarity is especially important in interdisciplinary or high-risk work, where reviewers may be less willing to infer missing logic.

## UKRI NERC Pushing the Frontiers: Current Call-Specific Findings

### Current Opportunity Status

The live call is:

- `Funding opportunity: Pushing the frontiers of environmental research`
- published `30 March 2026`
- opened `31 March 2026, 9:00am UK time`
- currently `open with no closing date`

This replaced the previous round-based mechanism. NERC’s applicant-led discovery science guidance says the scheme moved to an always-open model from spring 2026, with expert review followed by moderation panel assessment on a regular cycle.

### What the Scheme Is Trying to Fund

The official call says it supports:

- adventurous, ambitious, curiosity-driven environmental research
- projects that unlock new knowledge with “a degree of adventure previously unrealised”
- high-reward projects, which may or may not involve substantial risk

Applications may be:

- pure
- applied
- technology-led
- policy-driven

But they must still address, or provide the means to address, clearly defined environmental science questions.

This is important. “Frontier” here does not mean speculative without anchor. The project still needs a sharply defined scientific question and a convincing design.

### What Seems Most Important for Competitiveness

Based on the current call text plus the assessor guidance, the strongest applications are likely to do all of the following:

- make a very clear case that the work pushes beyond the current state of knowledge
- show ambition without sounding careless about delivery
- explain why the timing is right now
- show that the method is proportionate to the ambition
- demonstrate that the team and environment can support unusually challenging work

The older March 2024 assessor guidance is still useful as an interpretive aid, even though the delivery mechanism has since changed. It indicates that assessors are asked to focus on whether the research pushes the frontiers of knowledge and not to over-rely on metrics such as publications or income.

Inference: for this scheme, “frontier quality” is likely to be judged as a combination of intellectual ambition, potential to change understanding, and reviewer confidence that the team can manage the uncertainty.

### Scope, Eligibility, and Resubmissions

The current call requires the applicant to be:

- based at a UK research organisation eligible for NERC funding
- in a role meeting UKRI individual eligibility rules

The research must sit predominantly within NERC remit. NERC explicitly encourages applicants to contact them if the project is close to a remit boundary.

Uninvited resubmissions are not accepted.

### Size and Duration

The current opportunity states:

- maximum project cost is `£950,000 FEC`
- NERC funds `80% FEC`
- awards are usually `three to four years`
- there is no fixed duration, but the project should be designed realistically

Most applications are expected to be under the limit. If costs exceed `£950,000`, prior approval from NERC is required.

### Application Structure and What Assessors Will Read For

The current opportunity gives the clearest direct window into what a strong application must show.

#### Vision

Assessors look for work that:

- is of excellent quality and importance
- advances understanding or generates new knowledge
- is timely
- has impact on research, society, economy, or environment

Practical implication: the proposal needs more than a good idea. It needs a compelling “why this matters” case and a strong “why now” case.

#### Approach

Assessors look for an approach that:

- is effective and appropriate
- is feasible and addresses risk
- uses a clear methodology
- builds intelligently on prior work where relevant
- maximises outcomes and impacts
- shows the contribution of the research environment

NERC also explicitly expects:

- access to appropriate services, facilities, infrastructure, or equipment
- a detailed project plan with milestones and timelines in a Gantt chart

Practical implication: this scheme does not reward vague adventurousness. The bolder the idea, the more important it becomes to make the route to delivery concrete.

#### Applicant and Team Capability to Deliver

Assessors want evidence of:

- relevant experience appropriate to career stage
- the right balance of expertise
- leadership and management capability
- contributions to a positive research environment and wider community

This section uses the `R4RI` format and should be a narrative, not a standard CV.

Practical implication: the capability case should be built around what each person contributes to this project, not around generic prestige markers.

#### Ethics and Responsible Research and Innovation

The current call goes further than a narrow ethics box. Applicants are asked to consider:

- ethical and RRI issues in both topic and project design
- wider societal, environmental, and economic implications
- how positive benefits will be maximised and harms minimised
- data-related legal and ethical issues where relevant

Practical implication: if a proposal has environmental, societal, data, fieldwork, animal, human, or dual-use implications, these need to be treated as part of good project design, not as an afterthought.

#### Resources and Cost Justification

The current call asks for justification of more costly resources, especially:

- staff
- major fieldwork or collaboration travel
- equipment over `£25,000`
- unusual consumables
- facilities and infrastructure
- exception costs

Assessors are not looking for line-by-line accounting. They want to see that the requested resources are comprehensive, appropriate, justified, and represent optimal use of funds.

Practical implication: a good budget narrative is strategic. It shows why the requested resources are the minimum credible set for the science.

### Assessment Process

For the live always-open scheme, UKRI says:

- applications are assessed by expert review
- those meeting a quality threshold proceed to a moderation panel
- panel meetings occur on a regular cycle
- UKRI aims to provide a decision within `nine months of submission`

Important date clarification:

- the previous `January 2026` call closed on `21 January 2026`
- the current always-open opportunity opened on `31 March 2026`

The older `January 2026` call used a two-stage panel process with applicant response. The current live opportunity describes expert review plus moderation panel. I therefore treat the current call page as authoritative for process, and use the older assessor guidance only as supporting evidence for likely reviewer mindset.

## General Good Practice vs This NERC Call

| General proposal strength | What it means for NERC Pushing the Frontiers |
|---|---|
| Clear, important question | Must be a clearly defined environmental science question, even if the project is high-risk or exploratory |
| Originality | Must read as genuinely adventurous and ambitious, not just incrementally novel |
| Feasible method | Needs especially strong risk management and delivery logic because the scheme welcomes challenging work |
| Strong team | Must show the exact mix of expertise and environment needed for frontier research |
| Good funder fit | Must align tightly to NERC remit and the scheme’s curiosity-driven, high-reward purpose |
| Convincing impact | Should show why the work matters beyond the immediate subfield, while staying rooted in the science |
| Sensible budget | Must justify expensive resources without bloating the proposal |
| Clear writing | Particularly important because assessors must quickly see both ambition and credibility |

## Likely Reviewer Questions

These are my inferred reviewer-style questions for this scheme, based on the official criteria and assessor guidance:

- Is this genuinely pushing the frontier, or just a competent next step?
- Is the research question scientifically important and timely?
- Does the approach match the ambition of the question?
- Are the main risks understood and actively managed?
- Is the project exciting enough to deserve this scheme rather than a more routine responsive-mode route?
- Does the team have the capability, environment, and access to infrastructure to make this work?
- Is the budget proportionate to the likely scientific gain?
- If this works, how much does it change understanding or open new directions?

## Common Weaknesses to Avoid

- claiming novelty without showing exactly what is new
- using “high risk, high reward” as a substitute for methodological clarity
- proposing ambitious aims without a believable delivery plan
- underplaying remit fit or assuming NERC fit is obvious
- relying on reputation instead of making the team-capability case explicitly
- treating ethics, RRI, data, or facilities planning as peripheral
- writing a strong scientific case but a weak “why now” argument
- asking for expensive resources without a clear scientific necessity case

## Early Working Checklist

- Can the core research question be stated clearly in two or three sentences?
- Is the project visibly more adventurous than a standard incremental grant?
- Is the frontier claim specific and evidenced?
- Is the importance of the problem clear to a well-informed non-specialist reviewer?
- Does the method directly answer the question posed?
- Are the major scientific and practical risks identified and managed?
- Is there a credible three-to-four-year delivery path?
- Does the team narrative show capability tied to the actual work package needs?
- Is the project predominantly within NERC remit?
- Are any facilities, marine support, Antarctic logistics, HPC, or special approvals identified early enough?
- Does the budget look like the minimum justified cost of doing the science well?
- Would a reviewer finish the proposal believing both “this is exciting” and “this team can do it”?
